Phits vs Traditional Orthotics: What's Actually Different

Custom orthotics aren't new — podiatrists and physios have been moulding insoles from foot casts for decades. What's changed with Phits is the manufacturing process itself: instead of pressing or moulding a pre-cut blank around a cast of your foot, the insole is 3D printed layer by layer, directly from your footscan® gait data. That sounds like a manufacturing detail, but it changes several things that actually matter when you're wearing them.

Support is designed, not just shaped

A traditional orthotic's stiffness is mostly a property of the material and thickness you choose — the whole insole tends to flex more or less uniformly. Because Phits are built up layer by layer, the internal structure can vary block by block across the insole: firmer under the arch where you need control, softer under a pressure point that's causing pain, without changing the material or the outer shape. That's the practical benefit of "3D printed" over "moulded" — it's not really about the printer, it's that the stiffness map can follow your actual pressure data instead of being a single average.

The numbers that are genuinely different

  • Weight. Phits insoles are less than half the weight of a comparable traditional orthotic — noticeable if you're wearing them all day or running in them.
  • Accuracy. The printing process is accurate to within 0.1mm, with, in Gait & Motion Technology's own words, "no room for error in the manufacturing process" — there's no hand-finishing step introducing small inconsistencies between what was designed and what you actually get.
  • Repeatability. Because the design file is saved digitally rather than being a physical mould that can wear out or get lost, an exact replica can be reprinted later if you need a spare pair or yours wear out.

What they're made from

Phits are printed in Bluesint PA12, a material made from 100% recycled stock, and the manufacturing itself runs on 100% green energy at Gait & Motion Technology Ltd's UK facility. The insoles are finished with D3O® — the same impact-protection material used in sports and protective equipment — for shock absorption where it's needed most.

Does 3D printing actually mean "better"?

Not automatically, and it's worth being honest about that: a badly designed insole is a badly designed insole regardless of how it's manufactured. What 3D printing changes is the ceiling on precision and the ability to vary support zone by zone — it's the design and the footscan® data behind it doing the clinical work, with printing as the manufacturing method that lets that design actually get built accurately. That's why the process always starts with a Footscan Assessment: the orthotic is only as good as the gait data it's built from.
